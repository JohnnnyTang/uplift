# Reference landforms (session 7A)

One real example per boundary. The scene's shapes are **authored illustrations drawn from these
examples** (D-L2-1). They are not simulated and the page never says they form in the scene. Keys refer to
[sources.md](sources.md). Each section lists the facts we may cite, the shape cues the generator takes, and
what the scene leaves out.

All three are continental settings. Ocean ridges, trenches, subduction and volcanoes are out of scope
(D-L2-1, decision 3), even where a source discusses them alongside (for example magma in TDE-U's rift
passage).

## Convergent: continental collision, the Himalaya and Tibet

**Facts we may cite.**

- When two continents meet head-on, "neither is subducted because the continental rocks are relatively
  light"; instead "the crust tends to buckle and be pushed upward or sideways" (TDE-U).
- The Himalayas stretch 2,900 km along the border between India and Tibet (TDE-H) and reach 8,854 m above
  sea level at Mount Everest (TDE-U, its stated value).
- The neighbouring Tibetan Plateau has an average elevation of about 4,600 m (TDE-U).
- Beneath the Ganges Basin the crust–mantle boundary (Moho) is 40 km deep; beneath the Himalayas, 50 km;
  north of the Yarlung Tsangpo suture, 70 km, and it stays near that depth across the southern Lhasa
  block; beneath the Qiangtang block, about 65 km (all below sea level, Nab09). Adding the topography
  gives a crust about 70–75 km thick beneath southern Tibet, roughly twice the 35–41 km of ordinary
  continental crust (TDE-I, CM95).
- In that profile the Moho deepens fastest between about 150 and 250 km north of the Main Frontal Thrust
  (Nab09). So the Himalaya, from the frontal thrust to the suture, is about 250 km across at about 85° E.
- Mountain roots: thicker, lighter crust stands higher, as a floating block does (Airy isostasy; TS14,
  Fia). See [parameters.md](parameters.md#airy-root-proportion) for the proportion.

**Shape cues for 7C.** A range along the boundary with a high, broad crest and peaks; snow above a cited
snowline; the crust thickening into a root beneath it that is several times deeper than the range is
high; the lowland on both sides unchanged.

**Left out.** Erosion (deep valleys, glaciers), the plateau behind the range, the range's asymmetry (a
steep front and a high plateau behind), thrust faults and folds inside the range, and the 40–50 million
year history (TDE-H) as a timeline: the stage scrubber reads only "early → late".

## Divergent: continental rift, the East African Rift

**Facts we may cite.**

- The East African Rift is the 5,000 km long divergent boundary between the Nubian and Somalian plates
  (Sar14).
- Where continental crust is stretched beyond its limits, cracks open at the surface and a rift zone forms
  (TDE-U). The rift is a succession of fault-bounded basins (grabens), each about a hundred kilometres
  long and a few tens of kilometres wide (Cho05).
- Widths: the Main Ethiopian Rift floor is about 50 km wide; the Malawi rift 60 km; the Tanganyika rift up
  to 70 km (Cho05). The Kenya rift valley is about 60 km wide at its narrowest and widens to about 180 km
  further north (Kel94).
- Relief: shoulders stand about 1,300 m above the Malawi rift floor; the Nyanza rift floor is at about
  1,100 m with immediate shoulders at about 1,600 m; Lake Edward's floor is at 200 m with shoulders at
  2,300 m (west) and 1,600 m (east) (Cho05). So shoulder-to-floor relief ranges from about 500 m to about
  2,100 m.
- Faults: each basin has a major border fault accompanied by smaller normal faults and tilted blocks; many
  basins are asymmetric half-grabens (Cho05).
- Crust: along the Kenya rift axis the crust is about 35 km thick near Lake Naivasha and as little as
  20 km beneath Lake Turkana; under the flanks it is almost 40 km; the thinning is confined essentially to
  beneath the rift valley (Kel94).

**Shape cues for 7C.** A valley of real width sinking between stepped fault scarps; slightly raised
shoulders; the crossing ridges and streams dropped with the floor; on the front face, rock layers offset
at each fault and a crust that thins beneath the rift.

**Left out.** Volcanoes and magma, sediment and lakes filling the floor (rift infill is 3–5 km thick in
Kenya, Kel94), the usual half-graben asymmetry (the scene's steps are symmetric, a textbook
simplification), and opening rates (not verified this session).

## Transform: offset streams, the San Andreas at Wallace Creek

**Facts we may cite.**

- At a transform boundary plates slide horizontally past each other; crust is neither produced nor
  destroyed (TDE-U).
- The San Andreas fault zone is about 1,300 km long and in places tens of kilometres wide. The Pacific
  Plate has moved past the North American Plate at an average of about 5 cm per year; land on the west side
  moves north-west relative to the east side (TDE-U).
- The fault is right-lateral (dextral): it has accumulated about 300 km of right-lateral offset since the
  middle Miocene (SJ84).
- Wallace Creek, a stream crossing the fault in central California, has a present channel offset of
  128 m (SJ84). The average slip rate there is 33.9 ± 2.9 mm/yr over the past 3,700 years, and each of the
  past three great earthquakes moved it about 9.5–12.3 m (SJ84).

**Shape cues for 7C.** Ridges and stream valleys that cross the boundary are dragged in opposite
directions on the two sides, so every stream jogs at the fault; the jog is to the right when you look
across the fault (right-lateral, as at Wallace Creek). Relief and crust do not change.

**Left out.** The fault's real width and splays, sag ponds and shutter ridges, earthquakes, and any
vertical motion. The offset is compressed or enlarged for legibility: far larger than Wallace Creek's
128 m and far smaller than the fault's total 300 km (see [parameters.md](parameters.md)).
