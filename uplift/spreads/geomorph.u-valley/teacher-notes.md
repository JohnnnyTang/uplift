**Learning goal.** Learners see a glacier turn a river's V into a valley shaped more like a U, and explain two effects of rock type: soft rock wears away faster and deeper; hard rock keeps steeper walls. (Protocol; Results)

<!-- claims: C4 | sources: Model; Protocol -->

**Suggested use.** Open the explanation flap at any time; the question is optional and nothing is locked behind it. Ask learners to choose a rock and play. Pause, drag the timeline back, then slide the front face from under the ice to below the glacier's snout: where there is no ice, nothing wears the valley, and the V stays as it was. Then choose a second rock. It starts a separate run with another rock from the same starting valley; the first run is kept and its profile appears as a ghost line at the nearest recorded time. Changing the reading level, the camera, the cut or the ice view never changes a run. (Model; Protocol)

<!-- claims: C7, C13, C14 | sources: Protocol; Results -->

**What the claims rest on.** Every rock statement rests on the registered under-ice section and the registered runs, several seeds per rock. Soft rock is lowered faster and deeper at every matched model time. Hard rock keeps steeper walls after the same amount of erosion and at the end of the run; early on, granite has hardly been worn and its walls have not steepened yet, so a mid-run comparison may not show the wall difference. Floor width is not ordered by rock, so do not teach "the soft-rock valley is wider". A complete trough by every registered threshold is not reached in every rock; teach the change of shape, not a finished U. (Protocol; Results)

<!-- claims: C15 | sources: Lesson -->

**The default front face is a display choice.** The block opens with its front face on a section where all three rocks show a U by the model's own shape fit at the end of the run. That section was picked after looking at the runs, so it is a display choice, not a test; the claims rest on the registered section further up the valley. At the registered section, shale's U forms early and later opens into a broad trough as its weak walls keep failing. Margin notes average the section over a short stretch of valley and round lengths to a coarse step (both shown in the deeper level), so they change smoothly as the face moves. (Lesson)

<!-- claims: C9, C16 | sources: CS12; Lesson -->

**Overdeepened floors.** If the floor under the front face lies lower than the floor further down the valley, a margin note says the glacier has carved a basin there. Real valley glaciers carve such basins, called overdeepenings (CS12). In the recorded runs this note appears only in soft rock, in the lower part of the trough, and the basins are shallow. Near the valley head every rock, granite included, is lowered far more than lower down, where thick ice on a steeper bed slides fast, but the floor still rises towards the headwall, so the note does not appear there. (CS12; Lesson)

<!-- claims: C10 | sources: Model -->

**Time and scale.** Time is shown in model years, and erosion is compressed: each model year erodes as much as many years would without compression. Never convert model years into a real age. Magnitudes are indicative; the model is a simplified process model, not a forecast for a real valley. (Model)

<!-- claims: C8 | sources: Rocks; Sel80; Aug92 -->

**Common misconceptions.** "Glaciers only push rock out of the way": here the valley changes because sliding ice erodes the bed. "Hard rock is never eroded": granite is eroded, only more slowly. "Rock names fix rock strength": a heavily fractured granite can be weaker than a massive sandstone; the lesson's three rocks stand for soft, medium and hard rock masses. (Rocks; Sel80; Aug92)
