**Plates and boundaries.** Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into plates that move slowly relative to one another. Where two plates meet they move apart (a divergent boundary), towards each other (convergent) or past each other (transform) (TDE-U). This lesson shows the three where two continents meet; boundaries beneath the oceans are left out.

<!-- claims: PC3, PC4, PC5 | sources: TDE-U -->

**Collision.** When two continents converge, neither sinks, because continental rock is light: the crust buckles and is pushed up and sideways (TDE-U). The Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau are the classic example. Mount Everest rises about 8,850 m above sea level, and the plateau behind the range averages about 4,600 m (TDE-U).

<!-- claims: PC6, PC7, PC8 | sources: TDE-I; CM95; Nab09 -->

**Roots.** Continental crust is usually 30–40 km thick: seismic surveys give an average of about 41 km, about 30 km where the crust has been stretched and about 46 km under mountain belts (TDE-I; CM95). Under high ranges it is much thicker. Beneath southern Tibet the base of the crust, the Moho, lies about 70 km below sea level, against about 40 km beneath the Ganges plain to the south (Nab09).

<!-- claims: PC9, PC22 | sources: TS14; Fia -->

**Isostasy.** Thick, light crust floats higher on the denser mantle, a little like an iceberg in water. For crust of {{probe.crustDensity}} and mantle of {{probe.mantleDensity}}, Airy isostasy gives a root about {{probe.airyRatio}} times as deep as the mountains stand high (TS14; Fia). The drawing's root follows this proportion directly beneath the range: an authored relation with a source. Real ranges are also held up by the bending strength of the plate, which spreads their support more widely (TS14).

<!-- claims: PC10, PC11, PC12 | sources: TDE-U; Cho05; Sar14; Kel94 -->

**Rifts.** Where a continent is stretched, the crust breaks along normal faults and blocks drop down between them into a rift valley (TDE-U; Cho05). The East African Rift is a divergent boundary about 5,000 km long (Sar14); its rift valleys are typically about 50–70 km wide (Cho05; Kel94). Each basin is bounded by a large border fault with smaller faults and tilted blocks, many are lopsided half-grabens, and the shoulders stand roughly 0.5–2 km above the floor (Cho05). The drawing's steps are symmetric, a textbook simplification.

<!-- claims: PC13, PC21 | sources: Kel94 -->

**Thinner crust under the rift.** Beneath the Kenya Rift the crust thins from about 35 km in the south to about 20 km beneath Lake Turkana, and the thinning lies beneath the rift valley itself. The upper mantle there is unusually slow for seismic waves, a sign of hot, low-density rock, which the drawing does not show (Kel94).

<!-- claims: PC14, PC15, PC16, PC17 | sources: TDE-U; SJ84 -->

**Transforms.** At a transform boundary the plates slide past each other: crust is neither made nor destroyed, so the land is moved sideways rather than raised or lowered (TDE-U). The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform about 1,300 km long, in places tens of kilometres wide (TDE-U). It is right-lateral: looking across it, the far side has moved to the right, about 300 km in all (SJ84; TDE-U). Streams that cross it are bent sideways; at Wallace Creek the channel is offset by about 130 m (SJ84).

<!-- claims: PC15, PC18, PC19, PD2 | sources: TDE-H; TDE-U; SJ84; Params -->

**Real time scales, not in the drawing.** The drawing has no clock: its stage goes only from early to late. As cited facts about the real Earth, India collided with Asia about 40–50 million years ago, and most of the uplift of the Himalayas and Tibet came in the last 10 million years (TDE-H; TDE-U). The Pacific Plate moves past the North American Plate at about 5 cm a year on average (TDE-U); at Wallace Creek the offset built up at about 34 mm a year, over thousands of years (SJ84).
