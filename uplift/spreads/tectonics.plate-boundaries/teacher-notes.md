**Learning goal.** Learners name the three kinds of plate boundary and match each to the landform it makes where two continents meet: a mountain range with a deep crustal root where plates collide, a rift valley with thinner crust where they pull apart, and sideways offsets with unchanged crust where they slide past each other. (TDE-U; Nab09; Cho05)

<!-- claims: PD1, PD2, PD7, PD8 | sources: Params; Claims -->

**What the scene is.** The figure is an illustration: its shapes are authored, drawn from real examples, and labelled so on the page. Unlike the glacier lesson (From V to U), no landscape process is at work in this scene, and the landforms do not form there. Choosing a boundary, on the page or by dragging a plate's arrow on the block, goes back to the shared starting landscape; Play and the stage slider show the shape from early to late. Boundaries already viewed stay as labelled ghost outlines on the front face at the same stage. Changing the reading level, the camera or the front face never changes the scene. (Params; Claims)

<!-- claims: PC2, PD8 | sources: TDE-U; Params -->

**Suggested use.** Open the flap at any time; the question is optional and nothing is locked behind it. Ask learners to choose convergent and play, then divergent, then transform, and to describe the land and the front face each time. With all three viewed, scrub the stage and compare the ghost outlines: the range stands high where the rift lies low, and the transform leaves the outline unchanged. (TDE-U; Params)

**Real figures for discussion.** These are facts about real places, not measurements of the drawing. Mount Everest rises about 8,850 m above sea level, and the Tibetan Plateau averages about 4,600 m (TDE-U). The East African Rift is about 5,000 km long (Sar14), with rift valleys typically about 50–70 km wide (Cho05). At Wallace Creek, a stream crossing the San Andreas Fault is offset by about 130 m (SJ84).

<!-- claims: PC6, PC9 | sources: Nab09; TS14; Fia -->

**The prediction.** The answer is that the crust gets thicker. Light continental crust does not sink, so where continents collide it piles up into a deep root beneath the mountains, as under the Himalaya and Tibet (Nab09). At university level: the root is several times deeper than the range is high (TS14; Fia). On the front face it looks only about as deep, because heights and crust depths are drawn at different scales, both shown beside the figure.

<!-- claims: PD2, PD6, NT7, PC14 | sources: Params; Cho05; TDE-U -->

**Common misconceptions.** "The stage slider is a timeline": it orders shapes from early to late and shows no dates. "The drawing is to scale": heights are stretched and crust depths compressed, and the range and offset are resized to read at page size (Params). "Rifts are symmetric": the drawing's steps are, but real rift basins are usually lopsided (Cho05). "Transform boundaries change nothing": they leave the height of the land alone but drag ridges and streams sideways (TDE-U).

<!-- claims: PD3, PD4 | sources: Claims; TDE-U; Kel94 -->

**Left out on purpose.** The lesson covers continental boundaries only: volcanoes and magma, earthquakes, oceans and one plate sinking under another are left out, although plates also meet beneath the oceans (TDE-U). There is no erosion: real ranges are cut by rivers and glaciers, and real rifts fill with sediment and lakes (Kel94). If learners ask how long these landforms take, the deeper level gives cited real time scales; the drawing itself has none. (Claims)
